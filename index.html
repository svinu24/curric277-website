Expertise

ex·per·tise /ˌek-spər-ˈtēz/ noun

What does it really mean to be an expert?
Rethinking the word through Mark Chen's Leet Noobs (2012)

Presented by Your Names Here

Today is about one word: expertise. Mark Chen's book Leet Noobs follows a World of Warcraft raid group, but its real argument is that we've been defining expertise too narrowly. We'll take the definition apart and rebuild it using the reading, then discuss it.

The argument of the book

Chen wants to change what the word means

The everyday definition

expertise n.

A high level of skill or knowledge that one person has in a particular area.

It lives in your head, you prove it with tests, levels, and credentials, and you take it with you.

Chen's definition

expertise n.

“Successfully learning to participate in a community of practice.”

He moves away from “a cognitivist conceptualization of expertise as individual knowledge and skill acquisition.”

Today we rebuild it, one sense at a time. Expertise is… social earned situated collective sociomaterial contested
Left is the definition most of us walked in with, and probably close to the warm-up words. Reveal Chen's version: expertise isn't something you HAVE, it's something you DO with other people. How he studied it

  • Ethnography: Chen played as a rogue named Thoguht and was an officer in the Booty Bay Anglers guild.
  • He recorded chat, voice, gameplay video, and forum posts, because expertise has to be observed in practice, not measured with a survey.

“Being an expert in this case might not necessarily be tied to proficiency.”

Chen, describing himself as an expert gamer who admits he “kind of sucked” at shooters
Chen is a learning scientist who was also a member of the raid, so he's an insider, not a neutral observer. The quote is a great hook: he calls himself an expert gamer while admitting he isn't the most skilled player. He compares it to being "well read." Being "well played" is its own kind of expertise.

The game · World of Warcraft (Blizzard, 2004)

What expert play looks like in a raid

Tank

Usually a warrior. Holds the boss's attention and takes the hits.

Healer

Priests, druids, and shamans. They keep the tanks alive until they run out of mana.

DPS

Rogues, mages, hunters, warlocks. "Damage per second": they kill the boss.

Aggro & threat

Monsters attack whoever generates the most threat. If a DPS hits too hard, they pull aggro and die.

The endgame

At the level cap (60), the "real game" is raiding: 40-person dungeons, 4–5 hour nights, monsters reset every Tuesday.

Loot

Each boss drops only a few epic items for 40 people. Groups have to invent rules for who gets them.

wipe everyone dies · pull start a fight · leet elite / expert · noob newbie

This is our tie-in to the assigned game. Expertise in a raid isn't one person being great; it's 40 people each doing a specialized job at the same time. Keep this quick: the three roles, aggro, and scarce loot are all you need for the rest of the deck.

Sense 1 of 6 · Introduction & Chapter 1

Expertise is social.

Chen, quoting Collins & Evans: getting expertise is “a matter of socialization into the practices of an expert group.”

  • Community of practice (Lave & Wenger): you become an expert by learning to take part, not by memorizing.
  • New literacy studies: being “literate” in a group means you can produce, consume, remix, and critique its culture.
  • In the game: experts used fan sites and add-ons they only heard about from other players. WoW's lead designer: “The people that don't go to Thottbot are the casual players.”

“Just because you are good at your class, doesn't mean you are a good player. We value you as a player, not as a class.”

Thoguht (Chen), guild forum post, 2005
First sense: expertise is social. In WoW, knowing your character's abilities wasn't enough. What marked you as an expert was knowing the community's practices, like which websites and add-ons to use, and you only learned those by talking to other players. Chen's forum post makes the point: being good at your class doesn't make you a good player.

Sense 2 of 6 · Social & Cultural Capital

Expertise is earned.

You can't become an expert unless a community lets you in, and getting in takes two kinds of capital (Bourdieu):

Social capital: relationships and reciprocity, or “scratching one another's backs.”

Cultural capital: know-how from experience, the right tools, and recognized credentials.

“It takes capital to make capital.”

Even Chen was locked out

He played for over a year, and spent more than half a year trying to join a raid group, before he could even reach the endgame.

A level isn't expertise

A character's level is a credential, like a grade or a degree. Chen found it was “difficult to determine expertise by simply looking at the level”; players judged each other by grouping up and playing together.

Today: Destiny 2 raids have no built-in matchmaking, so you get in through Discord and LFG networks.

Second sense: expertise has to be earned through access. It isn't open to everyone equally. Social capital is who you know; cultural capital is what you know how to do, the tools you own, and the credentials you have. The level-60 point matters for school: a credential (a level, a grade) isn't the same thing as expertise. Chen also flags that this raises questions of who gets left out.

Sense 3 of 6 · Chapter 1: Individual vs. Group Expertise

Expertise is situated.

Stage 1

Leveling up

Levels 1–60. Mostly individual: you learn your class and set up your own add-ons and friends list.

→
Stage 2

Raiding

40 players and new rules. Using your abilities the way you always had now caused “catastrophic failure.”

“Leet noobs”

Expert players who become beginners again when the context changes. Their expertise didn't carry over automatically, so they had to relearn expert play together.

Adaptive expertise (Hatano & Inagaki): “the ability to generate new procedures to solve novel problems.”

“I feel like the game only just got interesting now that we are 60.” (a Booty Bay Angler, 2005)

Third sense, and the book's title: expertise belongs to a context. Level-60 players were experts at leveling, but raiding was practically a different game. They became "leet noobs," experts who were beginners again. So expertise isn't a fixed thing you carry around; it depends on where you are.

Sense 4 of 6 · Chapter 2: Communication, Coordination & Camaraderie

Expertise is collective.

  • Distributed cognition (Hutchins): like a navy ship's crew, the raid succeeded when players who each knew only their own role acted at the same time.
  • Its own language: chat split by role ([Raid], [madrogues]) and lingo only the group understood.
  • Failure as practice: wiping over and over was how the group found a fight's “groove.”
  • Trust: the raid came to see itself “as a single entity,” with each member trusted to do their part.

“This is how raids go. It's normal: You fight and fight and fight until your gear is broken, repair and do it again.”

Walt, Booty Bay Anglers officer

Chen's claim

Trust built on shared goals and real relationships was stronger than trust built on individual incentives.

Fourth sense: in a raid, no one person has the expertise; the group does. Chen borrows Hutchins's study of a navy ship, where the knowledge to steer the ship isn't in any single sailor's head. Walt's quote shows veterans teaching newer players that failing is normal. That's how a group learns.

Activity · Loot, merit, and expertise

Who has earned the epic?

The boss Golemagg drops one epic and 40 people want it. Every loot system is a theory of who deserves it. Which would your raid use?

What Chen's raid chose: the weighted roll, decided after three long forum threads. It doesn't reward the “best” players, on purpose: loot was “an added bonus to the more valued experience itself.” Chen compares grades to loot.

Read the four options and the "rewards" line on each, since that's the expertise connection: each system defines merit differently. Take a show of hands for each and click the cards to tally it. Then reveal Chen's choice. Most raids used DKP. Chen's group picked a system that deliberately didn't reward skill alone, because the expertise they valued was being a good member of the group. Seed for Q3: if grades are loot, what do grades reward?

Sense 5 of 6 · Chapter 3: Assembling to Kill Ragnaros

Expertise is sociomaterial.

Expertise is “not the ability to remember the exact numbers and ‘math’ underlying the game.” It's the ability to arrange people and tools so the work gets shared.

  • In March 2006 the raid adopted KTM, a fan-made add-on that showed everyone's threat in real time. Installing the raid's add-ons became required to join.
  • The leader stopped saying “melee, watch your aggro” and started calling out names: “Roger!”
  • Trust moved from people to tools, and the meter doubled as surveillance.

Fifth sense, Chen's headline claim: expertise is spread across people AND things. Press next once to "install" the add-on: the meter goes from question marks to live bars. The tool now does thinking the players used to do in chat. Then reveal the effects: more efficient, but trust shifted from people to the tool, and it doubled as surveillance. Blizzard later built threat displays into the game itself.

Sense 6 of 6 · Chapter 4: Death of a Raid

Expertise is contested.

The group decides what counts as expertise, and that can change. By 2006, raiding culture had shifted from know-how and trust to “artifacts and credentials”: meters, numbers, and loot.

A good raider is a good friend

“We're here to have fun, not be forced to do something, after all.”

Maxwell, raid leader

A good raider gets results

“People want loot. Here's how you get loot. You are not a bad person if you go somewhere just for loot.”

Helio, after the raid died

How it ended About a dozen core raiders leave for faster, DKP-based groups Progress stalls Sept 2006: a forum fight framed as “drama” ends the raid in days
Sixth sense: expertise is contested. The raid died partly because members stopped agreeing on what a good raider was. Maxwell's original vision: have fun, no one gets forced. Helio, after it fell apart: be honest that it's about loot. The trigger was a Sunday raid the allied guilds didn't know about, which became a forum flame war. Chen notes that "the simple act of calling it drama may have turned it into drama." His verdict: "We changed from being friends and family to just co-workers."

Putting it together

Expertise, rebuilt from the reading

expertise n. (after Chen, 2012)

  1. social: learning to take part in a community's practices
  2. earned: reached through access, which takes social and cultural capital
  3. situated: tied to a context; experts can become noobs again
  4. collective: held by a group, not just by individuals
  5. sociomaterial: spread across people and tools
  6. contested: defined, and redefined, by the community

Question 1 of 3 · Expertise is earned

Chen spent more than half a year trying to get into a raid. To him, expertise depends on who lets you in.

Think of something you're genuinely an expert at that no school would ever grade. How did you get into that community, and who let you in?

Follow-ups

Who might have been kept out of that community, and why?

Chen says a character's level is a credential, not proof of expertise. What “levels” in your life don't really show what you can do?

Ready0:00
An experiential question; everyone is an expert at something. Expect a game, a sport, cooking, a fandom, a music scene, or a job. Ask who their "sponsor" was, meaning the friend or older sibling who brought them in. That's social capital. If the room is quiet, go first with your own example. Follow-up stems: "Tell me more about that." "What about the reading made you think that?"

Question 2 of 3 · Expertise is sociomaterial

For Chen, expertise is spread across people and tools. The threat meter did thinking the players used to do themselves.

When a tool does part of the thinking (a threat meter, GPS, a calculator, AI), whose expertise is it, yours or the tool's?

Follow-ups

Where's the line between a tool that makes you an expert and one that replaces your expertise?

KTM made the raid faster but less close. Has a tool ever done that to a group you were in?

Ready0:00
This is where AI will come up, so let it. Push past "tools are good" or "tools are cheating." Chen would say knowing which tools to use, and arranging them well, IS the expertise: his add-ons "remembered things" for him so he could focus on strategy. But KTM also shifted trust away from people. If someone says the tool is just fair, ask: "Fair to whom? Who gets called out?"

Question 3 of 3 · Expertise is contested

Chen's raid fell apart when its members stopped agreeing on what a good raider was: a good friend or a good number.

In a class, team, or game you're part of, who gets to decide what counts as expertise, and do you agree with them?

Follow-ups

Chen compares grades to loot. If grades are the loot, what does school say expertise is?

Convince us: can a group be expert when no single member is?

Ready0:00
Connect back to the loot vote: each loot system was a theory of merit, and so is a grading system. Possible deciders: teachers, coaches, rankings, leaderboards, peers, the game's designers. For the last follow-up, point to the Ragnaros log: 40 people, none of whom could have done it alone.

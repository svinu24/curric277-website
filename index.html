Leet Noobs

The Life and Death of an Expert Player Group in World of Warcraft

Mark Chen (2012)

Presented by Your Names Here

Welcome everyone. Today we're covering Mark Chen's Leet Noobs, an ethnography of a 40-person World of Warcraft raid group. Roughly 12 minutes of summary, then we'll open it up for discussion. Use the arrow keys or space to advance.

Prologue · In the Fiery Depths…

Imagine 40 people in a volcanic cavern

Orcs, trolls, undead, and tauren crowd a lava-filled chamber. Some are dancing. Some are chatting about their day. One is assigning positions.

“Get in positions!” The fire lord Ragnaros rises from the lava:

“TOO SOON! YOU HAVE AWAKENED ME TOO SOON, EXECUTUS!”

Within moments, the raiders are all dead.

How he studied it

  • Ethnography: Chen played as a rogue named Thoguht and was an officer in the Booty Bay Anglers guild.
  • He recorded text and voice chat, gameplay video, and guild forum posts.

The lenses he uses

  • New literacy studies: being "literate" means participating in a community.
  • Actor-network theory & distributed cognition: people and tools do the thinking.
Chen is a learning scientist who was also a player in this group, so he isn't neutral. He says so himself: "take it with a grain of salt." The raid was led by Maxwell from a guild called The 7/10 Split, with players from several allied guilds.

The game · World of Warcraft (Blizzard, 2004)

A 60-second raiding primer

Tank

Usually a warrior. Holds the boss's attention and takes the hits.

Healer

Priests, druids, and shamans. They keep the tanks alive until they run out of mana.

DPS

Rogues, mages, hunters, warlocks. "Damage per second": they kill the boss.

Aggro & threat

Monsters attack whoever generates the most threat. If a DPS hits too hard, they pull aggro and die.

The endgame

At the level cap (60), the "real game" is raiding: 40-person dungeons, 4–5 hour nights, monsters reset every Tuesday.

Loot

Each boss drops only a few epic items for 40 people. Groups have to invent rules for who gets them.

wipe everyone dies · pull start a fight · leet elite / expert · noob newbie

This is our tie-in to the assigned game. Keep it quick: the three roles, aggro, and the fact that loot is scarce are all you need for the rest of the book. A "wipe" is when the whole raid dies and has to run back and try again.

Introduction · The big idea

Expertise lives in groups, not just in heads

“Expertise is not the ability to remember the exact numbers and ‘math’ underlying the game.”

Instead, expertise is the ability to arrange people, tools, and roles so the work gets shared out. Becoming an expert means learning to participate in a community of practice (Lave & Wenger).

Two kinds of capital (Bourdieu)

Social capital: relationships and reciprocity, or "scratching one another's backs."

Cultural capital: know-how from experience, the right tools, and recognized credentials.

“It takes capital to make capital.”

This is the thesis of the whole book. You can't become an expert raider by reading the numbers; you need access to a group that will let you practice. Getting that access depends on who you know (social capital) and what you know how to do (cultural capital), and each one helps you build the other.

Chapter 1 · Individual vs. Group Expertise

From solo skill to group expertise

Stage 1

Leveling up

Levels 1–60. Mostly individual: you set up your own interface, add-ons, and friends list.

→
Stage 2

Raiding

40 players with specialized roles, and constant negotiation over the roster, the schedule, and the loot.

“Leet noobs”

Expert players who become beginners again when the context changes. They knew the game, but they had to relearn expert play together.

“Just because you are good at your class, doesn't mean you are a good player. We value you as a player, not as a class.”

Thoguht (Chen), guild forum post, 2005
This is where the title comes from. Everyone in the raid was already a strong level-60 player, but raiding was a new kind of game: their individual skill didn't transfer automatically. They had to become a team.

Chapter 2 · Communication, Coordination & Camaraderie

A typical night in Molten Core

  • About 5 hours a night: gather and chat, pull, fight, wipe, talk it over, try again.
  • Chat split into channels by role (healers, rogues, tanks), and leaders moved to the main channel when it mattered.
  • Failure was welcome. Wiping was how you found a fight's "groove." Some players thought reading strategy guides ahead of time was a spoiler.
  • Bad nights got repaired on the forums the next day, and regular members reminded everyone: “we're family.”

“We're not a raiding guild… No one is getting left behind and everyone is getting a chance.”

Penny, Booty Bay Anglers

Chen's claim

Trust built on shared goals and real relationships was stronger than trust built on individual incentives.

Emphasize that the raid was mostly slow and social, not instant gratification. The "goal" the group kept stating was "hanging out and having fun." After one awful night in April 2006 (lots of wipes, bickering, a loot dispute), the group fixed itself on the forums. Remember that night; we'll compare it to the ending later.

Quick vote · The social dilemma of rare loot

Golemagg drops one epic. 40 people want it.

How should your raid decide? Vote with your hands, and we'll tally it.

Click a card to add a vote · shift-click to undo

What Chen's raid chose: the weighted roll, decided after three long forum threads. It's a little chaotic on purpose. Loyal raiders get better odds, but a newcomer can still win. It kept loot as “an added bonus to the more valued experience itself.”

Read the four options, then take a show of hands for each and click the cards to tally it. Then reveal what Chen's group did. Most real raids used DKP, which makes the competition explicit. Chen's group deliberately chose a system that made loot matter less. Plant the seed for discussion: Chen compares grades to loot.

Chapter 3 · Assembling to Kill Ragnaros

A new raid member: the add-on

Actor-network theory: the raid is a network of people and things (add-ons, voice chat, forums), and each one does part of the thinking.

In March 2006 the raid adopts the KLH Threat Meter (KTM), a fan-made add-on that shows everyone's threat in real time.

  • No more guessing: fights got faster and new bosses got easier to learn.
  • The raid leader stopped saying “melee, watch your aggro” and started calling out names: “Roger!”
  • Trust moved from people to tools, and the meter doubled as surveillance.

Then reveal the three effects. Fun fact: Blizzard later built threat displays into the game itself, so the players' tool changed the designers.

Chapter 4 · Death of a Raid

From “hanging out and having fun” to meltdown

  1. Late 2005

    Raid forms across several guilds, led by Maxwell of The 7/10 Split. Stated goal: hang out and have fun.

  2. Spring 2006

    KTM arrives and Ragnaros falls. The raid moves Molten Core to one night a week and starts a harder dungeon, Blackwing Lair.

  3. Summer 2006

    About a dozen raiders, mostly core members, leave for faster groups that use DKP. Progress stalls (“Vael breaks raids”), and even Molten Core gets hard again.

  4. Sept 10–11, 2006

    Matt sees the leaders' guild in Molten Core on an unscheduled Sunday without the allied guilds. He asks about it on the forum, accusations fly, and within days the raid is dead.

Walk through the timeline quickly. The trigger was small: a Sunday run that some members didn't know about. But it landed on months of built-up frustration. Maxwell's last post: "I thought of you as a friend, and now you treat me like this?"

Chapter 4 · Making Sense of the Meltdown

Same forum, same people, different ending

April 2006 · repaired

After an awful night of wipes, bickering, and loot fights, regular members (not leaders) restated the group's goals.

The conflict was framed as a normal family argument: temporary.

September 2006 · meltdown

Raiders framed the conflict as “drama.” A vocal minority set the tone, and many members didn't find out until days later.

“The simple act of calling it drama may have turned it into drama.”

Underneath it all Goals drifted toward loot & progress Progress stalled, then went backwards ~15 hrs a week for almost a year One raider's math: an epic cost “$133 in labor alone”
This comparison is the heart of chapter 4. The group had survived a crisis before, so what changed was the framing and the goals, not the people. Chen draws on Goffman's idea of framing here. The cost math is from a raider named Mandy, who priced raid time at $10 an hour and worked out what one epic item really cost.

Conclusion · The major assertions

What Chen wants us to take away

1

Expertise is collective. It's arranging people and tools well, not memorizing the math.

2

Access is everything. You become an expert only if a community lets you in, and that takes social and cultural capital.

3

Failure teaches, if the group frames it as a chance to reflect.

4

Trust shifted from trusting people to trusting tools and numbers.

5

Shared goals keep teams alive. When the goals split, so did the raid.

“We changed from being friends and family to just co-workers.”

Five takeaways, one sentence each. Chen's closing claim is that none of this is really about WoW: "Leet noobs can exist in any domain of activity." Any team of experts working on something hard (a lab, a startup, a group project) goes through the same thing.

Connections · “Leet noobs can exist in any domain of activity”

The same story, nearly 20 years later

Destiny 2 raids

Six-player raids with no built-in matchmaking, so players recruit through Discord and LFG sites. Your social network is literally your way in.

Overwatch Role Queue

Blizzard's team shooter now locks in tank, damage, and support slots. The role negotiation Chen's raid did by talking is now handled by the game's design.

Raider.IO & Warcraft Logs

Modern WoW players get public performance scores, and groups check them before inviting you. That's KTM's surveillance at a much bigger scale.

Outside games

Group projects, sports teams, jobs, clubs: specialized roles, trust, “loot” (grades, credit, promotions), and yes, drama.

Brief context for anyone who hasn't played these games. The point: everything Chen saw in 2006 has since been built into games and tools. Access through social networks, formal roles, and performance metrics are now the default. Then segue: "So let's talk about your own groups."

Question 1 of 3 · Friends or co-workers?

Chen asks game designers: “Do you want your players to be co-workers or would you rather they be friends?”

Think of a team you've been on: a raid, a group project, a sports team, a job. Was it more “family” or “co-workers,” and how did that shape what your team actually got done?

Follow-ups

Question 2 of 3 · When the meter joins the team

The threat meter replaced trusting teammates with trusting a tool, and it also became a way to watch people.

What tool in your life has taken over a job that used to depend on trust? Think read receipts, Google Docs edit history, Find My, a grade portal. How did it change the way your group treats each other?

Follow-ups

Where's the line between awareness and surveillance?

Convince us: would Chen's raid have lasted longer without the threat meter?

Ready0:00
Push past "tools are good" or "tools are bad." Chen says KTM made the raid more efficient AND weakened the family feel. Both are true. If someone says the meter just made things fair, ask them: "Fair to whom? Who gets called out?" Mention Raider.IO scores as the modern version.

Question 3 of 3 · Expertise school doesn't count

Chen argues expertise comes from getting access to a community, which takes social and cultural capital, not from memorizing facts.

What's something you're genuinely an expert at that school would never grade? Who or what let you into that community?

Follow-ups

If expertise depends on access, who gets left out, in games and in classrooms?

If schools took Chen seriously, what's one thing a class like this one would do differently?

Ready0:00
Expect answers like a game, a sport, cooking, a fandom, a music scene, or a job. Ask who their "sponsor" was, meaning the friend or older sibling who brought them in. That's social capital. This connects to the course's bigger theme of informal learning and to Chen's line that we should transfer social and cultural capital, not just skills.

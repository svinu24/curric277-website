Expertise

ex·per·tise /ˌek-spər-ˈtēz/ noun

What does it really mean to be an expert?
Rethinking the word through Mark Chen's Leet Noobs (2012)

Presented by Sufyan Siddiqui & Smyan Vinu

Today is about one word: expertise. Chen's book follows a World of Warcraft raid group, but its real argument is that we define expertise too narrowly. We'll take the word apart, rebuild it from the reading, and then open it up for discussion.

The argument of the book

Chen wants to change what the word means

The everyday definition

expertise n.

A high level of skill or knowledge that one person has.

It lives in your head, and you prove it with tests, levels, and credentials.

Chen's definition

expertise n.

“Successfully learning to participate in a community of practice.”

It lives in a group, and you prove it by taking part.

According to the reading, expertise is… social earned situated collective sociomaterial contested
Left is the definition most of us walked in with. Chen moves away from "a cognitivist conceptualization of expertise as individual knowledge and skill acquisition." For him, expertise isn't something you have; it's something you do with other people. The six words at the bottom are the six slides that follow.

Chen is a learning scientist who was also a member of this raid group, so he writes as an insider. The quote: he calls himself an expert gamer while admitting he isn't the most skilled player. He compares it to being "well read."

The game · World of Warcraft (Blizzard, 2004)

What a raid asks of its players

Tank

Holds the boss's attention and takes the hits.

Healer

Keeps the tanks alive until they run out of mana.

Damage (DPS)

Kills the boss without drawing its attention.

  • Raids are 40-person dungeons that took the group 4–5 hours a night.
  • A wipe is when everyone dies and the group tries again.
  • Bosses drop only a few rare items (loot), so groups invent rules for who gets them.
This is our tie-in to the assigned game. The key idea for the rest of the deck: no single player can beat a raid. It takes 40 people each doing a specialized job at the same time.

1 of 6 · Introduction & Chapter 1

Expertise is social.

Getting expertise is “a matter of socialization into the practices of an expert group.”

  • You become an expert by learning to take part in a community, not by memorizing facts.
  • In WoW, expert players used fan websites and add-ons they only heard about from other players.

“Just because you are good at your class, doesn't mean you are a good player. We value you as a player, not as a class.”

Thoguht (Chen), guild forum post, 2005
Chen quotes Collins and Evans here, and builds on Lave and Wenger's idea of a community of practice. Knowing your character's abilities wasn't enough. What made you an expert was knowing how the community played, and you only learned that from other players.

2 of 6 · Social & Cultural Capital

Expertise is earned.

You only become an expert if a community lets you in, and getting in takes two kinds of capital (Bourdieu):

Social capital

Who you know, and who will help you out.

Cultural capital

What you know how to do, the tools you have, and your credentials.

  • Chen spent more than half a year trying to get into a raid group.
  • A character's level is a credential, but it isn't proof of expertise. Players judged each other by playing together.
Expertise isn't open to everyone equally. Chen's line: "It takes capital to make capital." The level point connects to school: a credential, like a level or a grade, isn't the same thing as expertise.

3 of 6 · Chapter 1: Individual vs. Group Expertise

Expertise is situated.

Stage 1

Leveling up

Mostly on your own: you learn your character and set up your own tools.

→
Stage 2

Raiding

40 players and new rules. Playing the old way now caused “catastrophic failure.”

“Leet noobs”

Expert players who become beginners again when the setting changes. Their expertise didn't carry over, so they had to relearn expert play together.

This is where the book's title comes from. Level-60 players were experts at leveling, but raiding was practically a different game. Expertise belongs to a setting; it isn't something you carry everywhere.

4 of 6 · Chapter 2: Communication, Coordination & Camaraderie

Expertise is collective.

  • Like a ship's crew, the raid succeeded when each player did their own role at the same time.
  • The group failed together: wiping again and again was how they learned each fight.
  • Even loot was shared to favor the group, not just the “best” players.

“Trust based on shared goals and well-established relationships was stronger than trust based on individual incentives.”

Chen, Chapter 2
Chen borrows Hutchins's idea of distributed cognition: on a navy ship, no single sailor knows how to steer the whole ship, but the crew does. The raid was the same. Their loot system was a weighted dice roll that favored loyal members, chosen on purpose so that loot mattered less than playing together.

5 of 6 · Chapter 3: Assembling to Kill Ragnaros

Expertise is sociomaterial.

Expertise is “not the ability to remember the exact numbers and ‘math’ underlying the game.” It's arranging people and tools so the work gets shared.

  • In 2006 the raid adopted a fan-made add-on, the KLH Threat Meter, which showed everyone's threat live.
  • The tool took over thinking the players used to do, and trust shifted from people to the meter.

This is Chen's headline claim: expertise is spread across people AND things. Before the meter, players tracked threat by talking in chat. After it, the tool did that work. The raid got faster, but the leader started calling out individuals by name, and the meter became a way to watch people.

6 of 6 · Chapter 4: Death of a Raid

Expertise is contested.

The group decides what counts as expertise, and that can change. Chen's raid split over what a “good raider” was.

A good raider is a good friend

“We're here to have fun, not be forced to do something, after all.”

Maxwell, raid leader

A good raider gets results

“People want loot. Here's how you get loot. You are not a bad person if you go somewhere just for loot.”

Helio, after the raid ended

In September 2006 the group fell apart. In Chen's words: “We changed from being friends and family to just co-workers.”

After beating Ragnaros, more members cared about loot and progress than hanging out. Progress stalled, about a dozen core players left, and a forum argument framed as "drama" ended the raid within days. Chen notes that "the simple act of calling it drama may have turned it into drama."

Putting it together

Expertise, rebuilt from the reading

expertise n. (after Chen, 2012)

  1. social: learning to take part in a community's practices
  2. earned: reached through access, which takes social and cultural capital
  3. situated: tied to a setting; experts can become noobs again
  4. collective: held by a group, not just by individuals
  5. sociomaterial: spread across people and tools
  6. contested: defined, and redefined, by the community

“Leet noobs can exist in any domain of activity.” (Chen, Conclusion)

Read the six senses as one definition. Chen's point is that this isn't just about WoW. Any team working on something hard, whether a class project, a sports team, or a job, works this way. Then move to the discussion questions.

Discussion

Let's talk about expertise

  1. Think of something you're an expert at that school would never grade. How did you get into that community, and who let you in?

  2. When a tool does part of the thinking (a threat meter, GPS, AI), whose expertise is it: yours or the tool's?

  3. Chen's raid fell apart over what a “good raider” was. In your classes, teams, or games, who gets to decide what counts as expertise?

Chen, M. (2012). Leet noobs: The life and death of an expert player group in World of Warcraft. Peter Lang.

Take the questions one at a time and give people 3–5 seconds before jumping in. Q1 is about access (sense 2): ask who their "sponsor" was. Q2 is about tools (sense 5); AI will come up, so let it. Q3 is about who decides (sense 6). Useful follow-ups: "Tell me more about that." "What would you say to someone who disagreed?"
